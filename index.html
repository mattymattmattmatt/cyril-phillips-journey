A blacksmith from the Mallee

Four years, and the paper he left

Cyril Roy Phillips was born at Port Broughton on 9 August 1917. He enlisted at Adelaide on 6 July 1940, served as a tradesman with the army's field workshops, and was discharged at Wayville on 28 November 1944.

Those four numbers are from his discharge summary. Where a conclusion is only as strong as the dates lining up, this site says so, in the same words as the family booklet: "The evidence suggests", with a confidence and a Based on note.

Where the paper takes him

Dates of embarkation and landing are from his service record. The 1940 track via Fremantle and Colombo follows Moran's account of the South Australian sections. Lines between ports are indicative, as on the family maps. Damascus is a landmark on the Middle East sheet. The diaries quoted here do not place him there. From his own record · 1917–1940

Port Broughton, Lowaldie, Adelaide

On 25 June 1940 he was medically examined in Adelaide and found fit for Class I. He signed his attestation as a blacksmith of Lowaldie. On 6 July he was enlisted and sworn in as Private SX8216 and graded Group I Blacksmith from that day. He was 22, single, a Methodist. The army wrote down brown hair and blue eyes.

9 July: Recruit Reception Depot. The Mallee, and his mother Mildred Maud Phillips, were what he left.

NAA: B883, SX8216, pp. 1–5, 10, 18.

The evidence suggests

Woodside Camp, in the Adelaide Hills

strong · about 80%, for which section

The 2/2nd Army Field Workshops was a corps repair unit of the Australian Army Ordnance Corps. Moran writes that the Main Shop and No. 4 Recovery Section were raised in Victoria, and that Nos. 5 and 6 Recovery Sections were raised in South Australia and trained at Woodside.

Cyril marched in to the unit on 16 July 1940, in the 4th Military District, which was South Australia. At that moment the only parts of 2/2 AFW in the state were those two recovery sections. The timeline in the booklet marks his own training at Woodside as an inference: "Probably trains with the SA recovery sections."

Based on: NAA p. 5 (marched in 16 July 1940). Moran p. 9. The weight of the later evidence points to No. 6, which is set out when the route reaches Palestine.

From his own record · August–September 1940

Leave, then the train to Melbourne

11–19 August: pre-embarkation leave. On 16 August the unit's officers visited Adelaide. The diary says Nos. 5 and 6 Recovery Section were visited, but no parades were held, because most of the personnel were on leave. His dates match that sentence exactly.

14 September: he entrains for Melbourne. 15 September: he embarks for overseas. His mother, writing in 1943, remembered "the 14th Sept 1940", the day the train left.

NAA pp. 5, 10. AWM52 14/2/2/2. Moran pp. 9, 12.

The evidence suggests

Fremantle, and a ship the file does not name

high · about 90%

Moran: the two South Australian recovery sections entrained for Melbourne, joined the Main Shop and No. 4 Recovery Section, and embarked on the MV Nieuw Holland at Port Melbourne on 15 September. They called at Fremantle and Ceylon and reached Suez.

His record does not name the ship. He embarked on 15 September and landed on 12 October, the day after the convoy reached Suez. The Nieuw Holland sailed in convoy US 5, which left Fremantle on 22 September 1940.

Based on: NAA pp. 5, 10. Moran pp. 12–13 (ship, route, arrival 11 October). AWM photograph P01522.014, men of the 2/2nd "aboard the troop transport Nieuw Holland". Convoy US 5: Naval Historical Society of Australia.

What his unit was doing

A call at Ceylon, outward bound

The call at Colombo is Moran's account of the sections' voyage, not a port written on Cyril's own card. The same island comes back in 1942, and that second stay is on his record: he disembarked in Ceylon on 27 March 1942.

From here the 1940 track runs on to Suez.

From his own record · 12 October 1940

Kantara, as read

He disembarked in the Middle East on 12 October. The place is read as Kantara, on the Suez Canal. Moran says they sailed up the Canal to Kantara and went by rail to Helwan, near Cairo. It was the first time the whole unit camped together.

NAA p. 5. AWM52 14/2/2/2. Moran pp. 12–13.

What his unit was doing · October–December 1940

Helwan

The camp sat about fourteen miles from Cairo, on ground Moran remembered as the old quarries for the pyramids' limestone. The workshop's job, once the desert campaign began, was to keep vehicles, guns and the few British "I" tanks fit to fight.

Frank Hurley photographed the men with one of the mobile machine shops, the "L" trailer. The unit was here from October to December 1940.

What his unit was doing · December 1940 – March 1941

The coast road west

From Helwan the workshop moved into the Western Desert as corps troops with the British XIII Corps, as General O'Connor's offensive drove the Italian army out of Egypt. 5 December: Main Shop and No. 6 Recovery Section to Amiriya. 16 December: Fuka. 23–27 December: 6 Recovery Section forms the advance party at "Kilo 104", on the coast near Sidi Barrani.

January 1941: Bardia falls (3–5 January), then Tobruk is captured (21–22 January). A detachment of 6 Recovery Section "went in with the tanks", put five disabled tanks back into service under fire, and three men won the Military Medal: Sgt Allison, Pte Smith and Pte Dunning. That is the capture, in January. The siege came later.

February: 6 Recovery Section pushes on by Giovanni Berta and Tecnis. The Main Shop works at Maddalena, near Barce. 9–29 March: 6 Recovery Section at Mersa Matruh, 432 jobs, then back to Amiriya.

The evidence suggests moderate

If he was in 6 Recovery Section, Christmas 1940 was most likely at Fuka. Moran says the Main Shop worked there until 26 December and moved to Kilo 104 next day, "accompanied by the flexible 6 Rec. Sec." The diary also has an advance party at Kilo 104 from 23 December, so he may have been with that party instead. The unit's Christmas dinner at Fuka, seven courses and a bottle of Abbotts lager each, was photographed.

Based on: Moran p. 13. AWM52 14/2/2/2 (16 and 23 December 1940). AWM P01522.001 and P01522.003.

From his own record

Between October 1940 and June 1941 his card is blank. No wound, no illness, no transfer, no offence. He stayed with his unit. The Africa Star, on his later medal card, is the award for North Africa.

The evidence suggests · April–July 1941

Tobruk, and the windmill

probable · about 70–75%

In March most of the workshop went back to Egypt to prepare for Greece. No. 5 Recovery Section went to Greece. On 5 April No. 6 left Amiriya for Tobruk and arrived before the fortress was closed. For three months it was the only part of the unit inside, keeping vehicles and guns going.

The case that Cyril was with them rests on a hospital. On 14–17 June he was in "4 Aust Gen Hosp" and back with his unit the day he was discharged. The 2/4th AGH was inside the siege. There is a real caveat: his record never names the section, and twelve men of 6 Recovery Section who were on leave could not get back into Tobruk in April.

What his unit was doing · and where he was not

Greece, off this map, to the north

No. 5 Recovery Section went to Greece. Cyril's service record has no Greek service. That absence is one of the reasons the June hospital entry is read as Tobruk and No. 6, rather than as the section that sailed for Greece.

What was happening in the war

The Allied campaign in mainland Greece was fought in April 1941 and ended in evacuation. It is the reason the rest of his workshop had left the desert. It is not a posting of his.

Named in unit orders · July 1941 – February 1942

His name, at last, in the routine orders

11 July: 6 Recovery Section marches in to Barbara, near Ascalon, from Tobruk. Then Beit Jirja and Khassa. 27 August–12 September: the section services the 5th Indian Division at pumping station H4, on the oil pipeline in Transjordan. October: Ablah barracks in Lebanon, 3,000 feet up. November: Mafraq and H4 again, on road patrol. Winter, and more than a hundred frozen engine blocks to weld. 27 January 1942: the unit leaves Syria. 20 February: Port Said.

And the lines that name him:

  • 22 September 1941, Khassa. Duty section "6 Recovery Sec." Guard includes SX8216 Phillips, C.R., with men of that section such as K. Smith, MM.
  • 29 September, Khassa. Duty section "H.Q. & 6 Rec Secs". Phillips, and Dunning, MM.
  • 29 October, Ablah. Orders signed by Lt-Col Moran. Phillips on the guard for 30 October. That duty section was MT Section.
  • 6 November, Ablah. Duty section "H.Q. and 6 Aust Rec Sec". SX8216 Phillips C.R. on the guard.
The evidence suggests strong · about 80%

Three of the four guards were drawn from 6 Recovery Section, and on two of them he stands beside known members, including two of its Military Medal winners. Put with the South Australian enlistment, the voyage, and the Tobruk hospital, No. 6 is by far the most likely place for him.

Based on: the four Routine Orders, AWM52 14/2/2/7 and 14/2/2/8. Against: the 29 October guard came from MT Section. When the unit was broken up in September 1942, 5 and 6 Recovery Sections were kept "as complete as possible" in No. 2 Army Ordnance Workshop, but Cyril was posted to 6 Div Ordnance Workshop.

His record: embarks in the Middle East 12 March 1942, disembarks in Ceylon 27 March.

From his own record · March–August 1942

Ceylon

With Japan in the war, the 6th and 7th Divisions were recalled. Part of the 6th, including his unit, was landed in Ceylon against a feared attack. The unit embarked at Suez in the Westernland, Clan McTavish, Gogra and Cromarty, sailed on 13 March, called at Aden and reached Colombo on 27 March. His own card says he embarked on 12 March. It does not say which of the four transports he was in.

The first big Japanese air raid on Colombo came soon after they arrived. Moran remembered the camp, which he spells "Maddagedera", as hard work, intense heat, then torrential rain.

Homeward: disembarked at Melbourne "ex HMT Ekma". The handwritten date could be read as 4 August. The typed discharge summary and the unit diary both give 7 August. The diary says 13 July to 7 August were spent at sea.

From his own record · August–November 1942

Mangalore, then Greta

13 August–6 September: leave. 15 September: transferred to 6 Aust Div Ordnance Workshop. The same day, the diary says, "No. 1 Group" of that workshop moved from Minimbah to Greta, in the Hunter Valley. On 3 November he entrained for Queensland.

The evidence suggests moderate

He was in No. 1 Group at Greta. By July 1943 his record calls his section the "16 Bde Sec". It is likely that No. 1 Group was the 16th Brigade group. The diary seen so far does not say that in so many words. "Group" here is a workshop sub-unit. It is not the same thing as his trade grade, Group I.

Based on: NAA p. 6. AWM52 14/2/2/9, 15 September 1942.

From his own record · 8 December 1942

Townsville, the Duntroon

He embarked at Townsville on the Duntroon. Four days later he disembarked in New Guinea. From December 1942 the ordnance workshops were transferred to the new Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers, and their privates became craftsmen.

What was happening in the war

The Kokoda Track campaign was earlier in 1942, before this embarkation. The records do not place him on that track. He reached New Guinea as the fighting at Buna, Gona and Sanananda was coming to an end.

The evidence suggests · December 1942 – February 1944

Port Moresby

high · about 85%

He stayed fourteen months. On 12 July 1943 he was taken on strength by 2/121 Aust Bde Workshops, from the 16 Bde Section of 6 Aust Div Workshop. In September 1943 his mother addressed a letter to "Cfn C.R. Phillips, 2/121 Aust Bde Workshops A.E.M.E."

5–23 September: 128 Australian General Hospital, "NYD", then the New Guinea Details Depot, then back to the workshops. 29 January 1944: 2/1 Australian General Hospital, then at Bootless Bay, about 20 km south-east of Port Moresby. 1 February: re-graded Group I Fitter. 13 February: the hospital ship Manunda, at Port Moresby.

Based on: both hospitals were in the Port Moresby area, the Duntroon was the usual run from Townsville, and in July–August 1943 the 2/121 Brigade Ordnance Field Park at Port Moresby lists "2/121 Aust Bde W/shops" among the units it serviced. Caveat: the war diaries of the 16 Bde Section and of 2/121 Bde Workshops were not found online, so a detachment at Wau or Milne Bay cannot be ruled out.

From his own record · 1944

The Manunda, and home

17–22 February: Brisbane, No. 2 Australian Ambulance Train, 115 AGH at Heidelberg in Victoria, where essential hypertension is diagnosed, then 121 AGH, which had moved to Northfield in South Australia. 8–10 March: medical board, Class B. 25 March: he marries Irene May.

There was still a posting. 26 April–26 May with SA Lines of Communication Area Workshops. In June, Queensland: 7 Technical Maintenance Section, attached Advanced LHQ Signals, and Linemen Mechanics Course No. 70. By September he was back in Adelaide for discharge. 28 November 1944, Wayville, medically unfit.

What was happening in the war

Italy had entered the war on 10 June 1940. In September the Italian army advanced into Egypt. The British counter-offensive, Operation Compass, opened in December, as this workshop was moving up to the coast. Cyril's papers do not describe a battle. They describe a tradesman posted to the unit that repaired what the battle broke.

Chapter two · October 1940 – March 1941

Egypt and Libya

The unit recovered broken-down and captured vehicles, repaired trucks, guns and watches, and above all kept the few Infantry tanks fit to fight. "I" tank was the British name for them.

What his unit was doing

5 Dec 1940: Main Shop and No. 6 to Amiriya. 16 Dec: Fuka. 23–27 Dec: 6 Recovery Section's advance party at Kilo 104, near Sidi Barrani.

3–5 Jan 1941: Bardia falls. "All 6 Rec Sec" work at the 7th Royal Tank Regiment's lager. Captains Naismith and Redpath and their teams get 20 tanks fit for the next battle.

21–22 Jan: capture of Tobruk. A detachment went in with the tanks. Three Military Medals. 24 Jan: Main Shop to Ain el Gazala.

February: 6 Recovery Section via Giovanni Berta and Tecnis. Main Shop at Maddalena, near Barce. 9–29 March: Mersa Matruh, 432 jobs, then Amiriya.

AWM52 14/2/2/2 to 14/2/2/5. Moran pp. 13–22, 36.

Only if he was in 6 Recovery Section, and even then the advance party at Kilo 104 from 23 December is the alternative. The Mongareya Tavern photograph is the unit's Christmas, not a caption that names him.

Chapter three · April – July 1941

Tobruk: the siege and "the windmill"

On 5 April No. 6 Recovery Section left Amiriya for Tobruk. It arrived before Rommel's Afrika Korps closed the fortress. Moran: "The other absent member, '6 Recovery Section', was besieged in Tobruk. Their site was at the windmill and with craftsmen of the 2/1 Army Field Workshops and the LADs helped in the successful defence of the fortress."

What his unit was doing

8 April: 6 Recovery Section, under Captain J. W. Redpath, in Tobruk. 11 April: "No. 6 Recovery Section moved out to position on Derna Road about 1 mile West of Tobruk." 22 April: Redpath writes from Tobruk that the section has had no losses.

7–11 July: the section leaves. "Capt. Redpath and No. 6 Rec. Sec. marched in from Tobruk" to Barbara on 11 July.

AWM52 14/2/1/4 (2/1 AFW, 8 and 11 April). AWM52 14/2/2/5 and 14/2/2/6. Moran pp. 36, 41.

From his own record

14 June 1941: admitted to "4 Aust Gen Hosp" with influenza. 17 June: discharged (enteritis) and rejoined his unit the same day.

NAA p. 5.

The evidence suggests: he was in Tobruk during the siege probable · about 70–75%

The 2/4th Australian General Hospital was inside besieged Tobruk from 26 March to 21 October 1941. Being treated there, and back with his unit the day he was discharged, is only possible if the unit was close by. The only part of 2/2 AFW in Tobruk was No. 6. He had not gone to Greece with No. 5: his record has no Greek service. He is on 6 Recovery Section guard rosters later in 1941.

Based on: NAA p. 5 (14–17 June 1941). 2/4 AGH location: Our Contribution wiki. Moran pp. 36, 41. AWM52 14/2/2/7–8. Caveat: his record never names his section, and twelve men of 6 Recovery Section on leave could not get back into Tobruk in April 1941 (AWM52 14/2/2/5, 15 April). A 2/4 AGH admission register would settle it.

The evidence suggests: where "the windmill" was moderate

Moran does not say where the windmill stood. The only fix is the diary's "Derna Road about 1 mile West of Tobruk". Most likely a wind-pump or well-head on or near that road, just west of the town and well inside the perimeter. No wartime map marking it has yet been found.

Based on: AWM52 14/2/1/4, 11 April 1941. Moran p. 41. Still to check: AWM Tobruk maps (LIB52519, and the Italian 1:50,000 "Tobruch" sheet) and the 2/1 AFW diaries for May–June 1941.

242days the Australian War Memorial gives for the siege of the fortress, April to the night of 7 December 1941
94days from 8 April, when the diary places 6 Recovery Section in Tobruk, to 11 July, when it marched in at Barbara

The 94 days are the section's recorded stay, not a count of days proved for Cyril. Other histories end the siege on 27 November (231 days), when the garrison first linked up with the Eighth Army. The section had already left in July. The 242 is background, from the Memorial's dating.

What was happening in the war

The Memorial's account: from 10–11 April 1941 German troops cut the road, and the 9th Division and the 18th Brigade were among the garrison. Most Australians had left by the end of October. Supplied by sea, the "Tobruk ferry". In a 2011 Memorial film, historian Karl James tells the nickname this way: the propagandist known as Lord Haw-Haw said the garrison were caught like rats in a trap, and the men took the name. If Cyril was inside, the booklet says he would have qualified as a Rat of Tobruk. That was an association, not a medal, and the "if" is the 70–75% inference.

Chapter five · March – November 1942

Ceylon, home, and a new army

What his unit was doing

Embarked at Suez in Westernland, Clan McTavish, Gogra and Cromarty. Sailed 13 March 1942, called at Aden, Colombo 27 March. His card says embarked 12 March and does not name the ship. The raid on Colombo followed their arrival. Camp at "Maddagedera", as Moran spells it.

Moran p. 47.

From his own record

27 March 1942: disembarks in Ceylon. 7 August: disembarks at Melbourne ex HMT Ekma. A handwritten 4 August is the other possible reading. The typed summary and the diary agree on the 7th.

13 August–6 September: leave. 15 September: to 6 Aust Div Ordnance Workshop. 3 November: entrains for Queensland.

NAA pp. 5, 6, 18. AWM52 14/2/2/9, "the period 13.7.42 to 7.8.42 having been spent at sea".

What his unit was doing

After Melbourne: camp at Mangalore, Victoria, a march through the city with the 17th Brigade, then Minimbah and Greta in the Hunter. There the old army workshop was broken into a divisional workshop of a headquarters and two brigade-group workshops. On 15 September, the day his transfer took effect, No. 1 Group moved from Minimbah to Greta.

A note on the word "group"

The 6 Div Ordnance Workshop had a headquarters and two brigade group workshops, called No. 1 Group and No. 2 Group in the diary. By mid-1943 his record says "16 Bde Sec 6 Aust Div W/S", the section supporting the 16th Infantry Brigade. Those sections later became numbered brigade workshops. He joined the 2/121st in July 1943.

Do not confuse that with "Group I Blacksmith", which was his pay grade as a skilled tradesman.

AWM52 14/2/2/9. NAA p. 6. Moran p. 47.

The evidence suggests: No. 1 Group at Greta moderate

The transfer date is the day No. 1 Group moved to Greta, and by 1943 he was in the 16 Bde Section. The diary does not say, in the pages seen, that No. 1 Group was the 16th Brigade group.

Chapter six · December 1942 – February 1944

New Guinea

He arrived as the beachhead battles at Buna, Gona and Sanananda were ending, and he stayed fourteen months. From December 1942 the workshops moved to the Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers. A private in those units became a craftsman. In September 1943 his mother knew the new title. She wrote to "Cfn".

The evidence suggests: the Port Moresby area high · about 85%

The Duntroon from Townsville was the usual run to Moresby. 128 AGH was at Port Moresby. 2/1 AGH was at Bootless Bay from September 1943 to June 1944. The 2/121 Brigade Ordnance Field Park at Port Moresby lists the workshops among units it serviced in July–August 1943. He was evacuated from Port Moresby.

The evidence suggests: what the 2/121st supported moderate

The 2/121 Brigade Ordnance Field Park had previously been the "17 Bde Section, 6 Div OFP". That suggests the 2/121st brigade units, the workshop included, were tied to the 6th Division's 17th Brigade, which fought at Wau and Salamaua in 1943. It is not confirmed for the workshop itself.

Based on: AWM52 13/40/13/1, title and opening entries.

Worth knowing for the family

The six ticks are what this service would be expected to earn: 1939–45 Star and Africa Star for the Middle East, Pacific Star for New Guinea, and the Defence Medal, War Medal and Australia Service Medal for the service as a whole. If he was in the siege, he would also have qualified as a Rat of Tobruk. A next of kin can ask Defence Honours and Awards to confirm entitlement. Replica sets can be bought for a family display. They are not the originals.

His service record

Press a stamp

These are the abbreviations a grandchild meets in the file. The stamp opens the note. The scans are the pages themselves. Select a photograph to enlarge it, then pinch or use the zoom buttons.

SX8216

His regimental number. Reading the number, as a guide rather than a line in the file: the first letter is the state of enlistment, S for South Australia, and X marked a man of the Second AIF. 8216 is his own number. It is on the attestation, the casualty form, the medal card and his mother's letter.

Group I Blacksmith, later Group I Fitter

Skilled tradesmen were paid an extra rate by trade group. Group I was the top grade. He was a Group I Blacksmith from enlistment. On 1 February 1944 he was re-graded Group I Fitter. In a field workshop the blacksmiths forged and welded: springs, axles, recovery gear, oxy-acetylene cutting. Fitters machined and assembled parts. "Ins" after Fitter is not spelled out in the file. It may mean instrument.

This is not "No. 1 Group", which was a brigade workshop at Greta. See the glossary.

Cfn, Craftsman

The rank AEME used instead of Private. His mother's letter of September 1943 already says Cfn. The 1977 war-graves card says Craftsman.

4 AGH, and the other hospitals

"4 Aust Gen Hosp" in June 1941 is the 2/4th Australian General Hospital of the Second AIF. The booklet's Tobruk inference depends on that hospital being inside the siege. Later he is in 128 AGH (Port Moresby), 2/1 AGH (Bootless Bay), 115 AGH (Heidelberg), 121 AGH (Northfield) and 105 AMH (Adelaide). AGH is Australian General Hospital. AMH is Australian Military Hospital.

NYD, and the X list

NYD is "not yet diagnosed", written on an admission before the doctors had decided. Men temporarily away from their unit, usually in hospital, went to the X list and were struck off it when they returned. His June 1941 and September 1943 absences are that kind of entry.

Medical classes

Class I at enlistment meant fit for general service. In 1944 the boards moved him through Class B (fit for limited duties), Temporary D, and a final Class D on 17 October: unfit for service. That is why the discharge on 28 November says medically unfit. The illness named at Heidelberg is essential hypertension, high blood pressure.

HMT, and the ships that are named

HMT is His Majesty's Transport, a requisitioned troopship. His record names three ships: HMT Ekma into Melbourne in August 1942, the Duntroon from Townsville in December 1942, and the hospital ship Manunda from Port Moresby in February 1944. The 1940 ship is not named. The March 1942 ship, of the four the unit used, is not named either.

AAOC, AEME, RAEME

The workshops began in the Australian Army Ordnance Corps. On 1 December 1942 they transferred to the Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers. In 1948 that corps became the Royal Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers. His graves card uses the later title. A LAD was a light aid detachment, a small repair team with a fighting unit. An OFP was an ordnance field park, the spare-parts column. L of C was a Lines of Communication Area, the state administrative command. LHQ was Land Headquarters. 1 ATTD is left as the file writes it.

The pages

A guide to the records

What the trade terms mean

Army Field Workshop

A large mobile repair unit for a corps. The 2/2nd had a Main Shop, with machine-tool trailers and engineering, transport, instrument and radio sections, and three Recovery Sections, Nos. 4, 5 and 6.

Recovery Section

The most mobile part. It brought in broken, bogged, damaged or captured vehicles, tanks and guns, often close to the fighting, and repaired what it could on the spot. No. 6 was raised in South Australia.

Group I

The top tradesman's pay grade. Not the same as No. 1 Group, which was a workshop sub-unit at Greta in 1942.

Blacksmith and fitter

Blacksmiths forged and welded. Fitters machined and assembled. "Ins" beside Fitter may mean instrument. The file does not say.

Craftsman

AEME's word for a private. From 1943 he is Cfn. In 1977 the graves card says Craftsman.

AAOC, AEME, RAEME

Ordnance Corps, then from 1 December 1942 the Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers, then from 1948 the Royal Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers.

Brigade workshop

After 1942 the big workshops were split for jungle war into brigade groups, later numbered. The 2/121st is his from July 1943.

LAD and OFP

A light aid detachment lived with a combat unit. An ordnance field park carried the spare parts. In Tobruk, recovery sections and LADs worked side by side. That is Moran's sentence, not a claim about a particular hour of Cyril's.

X list and NYD

X list: away from the unit, usually in hospital. NYD: not yet diagnosed.

Classes A to D

Fitness. Class B, limited duties. Class D, unfit, which is the discharge.

L of C, HMT, LHQ

Lines of Communication Area. His Majesty's Transport. Land Headquarters. 4 Military District was South Australia's army district, the one he marched into in July 1940.

"As read"

Where the booklet could not be sure of a handwritten word, it says so. Kantara, the Ekma date, and "Posted 8/4/54" are the three to remember.
