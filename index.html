A blacksmith from the Mallee

Four years, and the paper he left

Cyril Roy Phillips was born at Port Broughton on 9 August 1917. He enlisted at Adelaide on 6 July 1940, served as a tradesman with the army's field workshops, and was discharged at Wayville on 28 November 1944.

1,607days of service
1,129of them overseas
692Middle East and Ceylon
437in Papua

The record gives 1,607 days in all and 1,129 overseas. Counted the Army’s way, inclusively, that overseas total is 692 days from embarkation on 15 September 1940 to Melbourne on 7 August 1942, plus 437 days from Townsville on 8 December 1942 to Brisbane on 17 February 1944.

His family in the records

Mother: Mildred Maud Phillips, first at Lowaldie. By September 1943 she was at Borrika, writing for the Female Relative Badge, the badge “To the Women of Australia” issued to the nearest female relative of a man serving overseas. The file is marked “Badge sent”.

Wife: Irene May. Those are her given names. They married on 25 March 1944, and the form was amended on 28 April 1944 to make her next of kin, address Borrika. In 1977 she was living at Mile End. A family-tree lead suggests the surname Dohnt. No civil record confirming it was found for the fact-checked edition, so it is not printed as fact.

Where the paper takes him

Dates of embarkation and landing are from his service record. The 1940 track via Fremantle and Colombo follows Moran's account of the South Australian sections. Lines between ports are indicative, as on the family maps. Damascus is a landmark on the Middle East sheet. The diaries quoted here do not place him there. From his own record · 1917–1940

Port Broughton, Lowaldie, Adelaide

On 25 June 1940 he was medically examined in Adelaide and found fit for Class I. He signed his attestation as a blacksmith of Lowaldie. On 6 July he was enlisted and sworn in as Private SX8216 and graded Group I Blacksmith from that day. He was 22, single, a Methodist. The army wrote down brown hair and blue eyes.

9 July: Recruit Reception Depot. The Mallee, and his mother Mildred Maud Phillips, were what he left.

NAA: B883, SX8216, pp. 1–5, 10, 18.

Established · his section

Woodside, and 6 Australian Recovery Section

The 2/2nd Army Field Workshops had a Main Shop and three Recovery Sections. The Main Shop and No. 4 were raised in Victoria. Nos. 5 and 6 were raised in South Australia and trained at Woodside Camp in the Adelaide Hills. On 16 August 1940 the war diary records a visit to Nos. 5 and 6, when most of the men were on pre-embarkation leave. Cyril’s own leave was 11–19 August.

In a letter of 8 February 1971, fellow soldier Bert Evans (SX4626) identifies him as “SX 8216 Cfn C R Phillips of 6 Aust Recovery Section”, and says he served with Cyril from June 1940 until September 1942. That named identification replaces the earlier estimate that he was only “about 80%” likely to belong to one of the two South Australian sections.

Based on: NAA p. 5. Moran p. 9. AWM52 14/2/2/2, 16 August 1940. Evans’s letter, transcript on Virtual War Memorial Australia, submitted by Joan Soroka. The letter opens by saying Cyril himself had asked Evans to record and verify these events.

From his own record · August–September 1940

Leave, then the train to Melbourne

11–19 August: pre-embarkation leave. On 16 August the unit's officers visited Adelaide. The diary says Nos. 5 and 6 Recovery Section were visited, but no parades were held, because most of the personnel were on leave. His dates match that sentence exactly.

14 September: he entrains for Melbourne. 15 September: he embarks for overseas. His mother, writing in 1943, remembered "the 14th Sept 1940", the day the train left.

NAA pp. 5, 10. AWM52 14/2/2/2. Moran pp. 9, 12.

The evidence suggests

Fremantle, and a ship the file does not name

very probably

His record does not name the ship. It says he entrained for Melbourne on 14 September and embarked on 15 September. His mother, in 1943, wrote that he “sailed on the 14th Sept 1940”. She was probably remembering the day he left South Australia.

Brigadier Moran’s unit history names the transport as the Dutch liner Nieuw Holland. The Naval Historical Society of Australia records that convoy US 5 sailed from Fremantle on 22 September 1940 with four Dutch liners, Christiaan Huygens, Indrapoera, Nieuw Holland and Slamat, carrying 4,262 troops, escorted by HMAS Canberra as far as 2 October. He therefore boarded at Melbourne, called at Fremantle, and sailed with the convoy from there.

The evidence suggests still to check

The Department of Veterans’ Affairs order of battle records an earlier party of the 2/2nd Field Workshops leaving Melbourne on 13 August 1940 and travelling via Bombay, also reaching the Middle East on 12 October. Cyril was on leave until 19 August, so he cannot have been in that party. The unit evidently travelled in two groups. Moran’s statement that the South Australian sections joined the Main Shop at Melbourne should be checked against the war diary.

Based on: NAA pp. 5, 10. Moran. Naval Historical Society, “Australian Naval History on 22 September 1940”. DVA order of battle.

What his unit was doing

A call at Ceylon, outward bound

The call at Colombo is Moran's account of the sections' voyage, not a port written on Cyril's own card. The same island comes back in 1942, and that second stay is on his record: he disembarked in Ceylon on 27 March 1942.

From here the 1940 track runs on to Suez.

From his own record · 12 October 1940

Kantara, as read

He disembarked in the Middle East on 12 October. The place is read as Kantara, on the Suez Canal. Moran says they sailed up the Canal to Kantara and went by rail to Helwan, near Cairo. It was the first time the whole unit camped together.

NAA p. 5. AWM52 14/2/2/2. Moran pp. 12–13.

What his unit was doing · October–December 1940

Helwan

The camp sat about fourteen miles from Cairo, on ground Moran remembered as the old quarries for the pyramids' limestone. The workshop's job, once the desert campaign began, was to keep vehicles, guns and the few British "I" tanks fit to fight.

Frank Hurley photographed the men with one of the mobile machine shops, the "L" trailer. The unit was here from October to December 1940.

What his unit was doing · December 1940 – March 1941

The coast road west

From Helwan the workshops moved into the Western Desert in December 1940 as the British and Commonwealth offensive drove the Italian Tenth Army out of Egypt. The diary records the Main Shop and No. 6 Recovery Section at Amiriya on 5 December and Fuka on 16 December. An advance party of No. 6 was at Kilo 104, near Sidi Barrani, from 23 December, so we cannot say exactly where Cyril spent Christmas.

The 6th Australian Division attacked Bardia on 3 January 1941. The town fell on 4 January and the last Italian positions surrendered on 5 January. Evans recalled Cyril, as blacksmith, working “extremely long and sustained hours” with others to make damaged British and Italian tanks serviceable for the assault on Tobruk. Italian tanks captured at Bardia were issued to A Squadron of the 6th Division Cavalry, which painted large white kangaroos on hulls and turrets so they would not be mistaken for the enemy. They went into action around Tobruk, captured by the 6th Division on 21–22 January. The tanks Cyril helped make serviceable were very probably among these.

Evans also names where the section kept 6th Division vehicles running: before and in Tobruk, then at Derna, “Sholta” (almost certainly Slonta), Barce and Maddalena. In March, at Mersa Matruh, he said Cyril and his section “set up” 140 sets of vehicle springs, working almost non-stop. Evans was the unit’s R&I clerk. The diary records 432 jobs for No. 6 Recovery Section there in February–March 1941. The 140 is his recollection, thirty years later, and should stay attributed to him unless a repair ledger is found.

One South Australian of the same unit was decorated for this campaign: Private Ernest Oswald James Dunning, SX9731, 2/2 Field Workshops, Military Medal, gazetted 8 July 1941. His SX number suggests one of the two South Australian recovery sections. His section is not yet confirmed.

From his own record

Between October 1940 and June 1941 his card is blank. No wound, no illness, no transfer, no offence. He stayed with his unit. The Africa Star, on his later medal card, is the award for North Africa.

Established · April–July 1941

Tobruk, during the siege

In early March 1941 the newly arrived 9th Australian Division began relieving the 6th in Cyrenaica, so the 6th could go to Greece. When Rommel struck at the end of March, the 9th fell back and reached Tobruk on 9 April. Axis forces cut the landward approaches on 10–11 April. That is when the Australian War Memorial dates the siege. It was not a March handover of the fortress.

Evans says that within a week of returning to Alexandria from Mersa Matruh the unit was ordered back with the 9th Division, and re-entered Tobruk a few hours before it was invested. No. 6 Recovery Section, under Captain J. W. Redpath, was the 2/2nd’s element sent there. On 11 April the 2/1st Army Field Workshops diary places the section on the Derna Road about one mile west of Tobruk. Moran calls the site “the windmill”. It has not been matched to a feature on a contemporary map.

His own record: admitted to “4 Aust Gen Hosp” on 14 June with influenza, discharged on 17 June with enteritis, and back with his unit the same day. The 2/4th Australian General Hospital was inside the siege. The Rats of Tobruk Association register lists him by name and number, with a note referring to 4 AGH in Tobruk. “Rat of Tobruk” is a veteran identity, not one of the six medals on his card.

The Derna Road map, the destroyer, and the diary pages

What his unit was doing · and where he was not

Greece, off this map, to the north

The 6th Division, which his section had been keeping on the road, was withdrawn for Greece. No. 5 Recovery Section of his own unit went. Cyril’s service record has no Greek service. Evans and the later records place him with No. 6, which went back into Tobruk.

What was happening in the war

The Allied campaign in mainland Greece was fought in April 1941 and ended in evacuation. It is not a posting of his.

Named in unit orders · July 1941 – February 1942

His name, at last, in the routine orders

11 July: 6 Recovery Section marches in to Barbara, near Ascalon, from Tobruk. Then Beit Jirja and Khassa. 27 August–12 September: the section services the 5th Indian Division at pumping station H4, on the oil pipeline in Transjordan. October: Ablah barracks in Lebanon, 3,000 feet up. November: Mafraq and H4 again, on road patrol. Winter, and more than a hundred frozen engine blocks to weld. 27 January 1942: the unit leaves Syria. 20 February: Port Said.

And the lines that name him:

  • 22 September 1941, Khassa. Duty section "6 Recovery Sec." Guard includes SX8216 Phillips, C.R., with men of that section such as K. Smith, MM.
  • 29 September, Khassa. Duty section "H.Q. & 6 Rec Secs". Phillips, and Dunning, MM.
  • 29 October, Ablah. Orders signed by Lt-Col Moran. Phillips on the guard for 30 October. That duty section was MT Section.
  • 6 November, Ablah. Duty section "H.Q. and 6 Aust Rec Sec". SX8216 Phillips C.R. on the guard.

These orders name him. They do not have to carry the old “about 80%” guess: Evans had already identified the section. One roster places him on an MT Section duty, a reminder that men could be detailed outside their own section. Evans remembered the unit “keeping Indian vehicle convoys moving on their journey to Iraq”. The Iraqi revolt had ended in May 1941, before the section left Tobruk, so the convoys of August–September belonged to the later build-up in Iraq and Persia. Evans also lists Syria before Transjordan. The diary order, Palestine, then Transjordan, then Lebanon, is the one followed here.

His record: embarks in the Middle East 12 March 1942, disembarks in Ceylon 27 March.

From his own record · March–August 1942

Ceylon

With Japan in the war, the 6th and 7th Divisions were recalled. On the voyage home the 6th Division’s 16th and 17th Brigades were landed in Ceylon as an interim garrison. The 2/2nd Army Field Workshops reached Colombo on 27 March 1942. Moran spells the camp “Maddagedera”. Japanese carrier aircraft raided Colombo on Easter Sunday, 5 April 1942, nine days after the unit landed. The unit had embarked at Suez in the Westernland, Clan McTavish, Gogra and Cromarty. His card says he embarked on 12 March and does not name which ship.

Homeward he is named: HMT Ekma, sailed 13 July 1942, Melbourne on 7 August. A handwritten date could be misread as 4 August. The typed discharge summary and the unit diary support 7 August. Units of the 16th Brigade, such as the 2/1st Battalion, left Ceylon on 13 July and landed at Melbourne on 7 August, the same dates as his. Units of the 17th Brigade, such as the 2/5th Battalion on Athlone Castle, arrived on 4 August. He came home with the 16th Brigade group. That fits his later posting to the 16 Brigade Section. Evans’s “17th Brigade” is either the outward attachment or a slip of memory.

From his own record · August–November 1942

Mangalore, then Greta

13 August–6 September: leave. 15 September: transferred to 6 Aust Div Ordnance Workshop. The same day, the diary says, "No. 1 Group" of that workshop moved from Minimbah to Greta, in the Hunter Valley. On 3 November he entrained for Queensland.

The evidence suggests unproven

He may have been in No. 1 Group. The diary records that group moving from Minimbah to Greta on the day of his transfer, and by July 1943 his record calls his section the “16 Bde Sec”. The diary does not prove the group. “No. 1 Group” is a workshop sub-unit. It is not his trade grade, Group I. On 1 December 1942 the workshops passed from the Australian Army Ordnance Corps to the new Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers. Craftsman is the AEME title for a private, not a promotion.

Based on: NAA p. 6. AWM52 14/2/2/9, 15 September 1942.

From his own record · 8 December 1942

Townsville, the Duntroon

He embarked at Townsville on the Duntroon on 8 December 1942 and disembarked in New Guinea on 12 December. The record says New Guinea. It does not name the port.

What was happening in the war

The Papuan campaign was not over. Gona had fallen on 9 December, but Buna held until 2 January 1943 and Sanananda until 22 January. He arrived during the closing phase of the beachhead battles. The 16th Brigade had fought on the Kokoda Track and at Sanananda. It was flown back to Port Moresby and returned to Australia in January 1943. His record does not place him on the Track. He stayed on.

Strongly indicated · December 1942 – February 1944

The Port Moresby area

strongly indicated

On arrival his record places him with the 16 Brigade Section of the 6 Australian Division Workshop. On 12 July 1943 he was taken on strength by 2/121 Australian Brigade Workshops from that section. His mother’s letter of 6 September 1943 addresses “Cfn C.R. Phillips, 2/121 Aust Bde Workshops A.E.M.E.” and writes “Australia (now)” under the unit. Whether that was a postal habit, a misunderstanding, or something in his letters home is still open.

The strongest geographical conclusion is the Port Moresby base area, in the Territory of Papua. He was admitted to 128 Australian General Hospital there from 5 to 23 September 1943, marked NYD, then the New Guinea Details Depot, then back to his unit. On 29 January 1944 he was admitted to 2/1 Australian General Hospital at Bootless Bay, outside Port Moresby. Both hospitals check out: the 128th served at Port Moresby until August 1944, and the 2/1st had a 1,200-bed hospital at Bootless Bay from September 1943. On 1 February he was regraded Group I Fitter. The word after Fitter is “Ins”. “Instrument” is plausible and unconfirmed. On 13 February he embarked at Port Moresby on the hospital ship Manunda.

Based on: NAA pp. 6 and 10. AWM52 13/40/13/1. Hospital histories. This does not prove he never went elsewhere in New Guinea, that the workshop stood inside the town, or that all of 2/121 Brigade Workshops was based there. The section and workshop war diaries have not been located. The safe wording is “Port Moresby area, strongly indicated”.

From his own record · 1944

The Manunda, and home

He reached Brisbane on 17 February 1944, then No. 2 Australian Ambulance Train to 115 AGH at Heidelberg, where essential hypertension was diagnosed on 19 February, then 121 AGH at Northfield. That hospital had moved there from Katherine in August 1943 and occupied the Morris Hospital site. A board on 8–10 March graded him Class B. On 25 March he married Irene May. On 28 April she was entered as next of kin.

There was still a posting. 26 April–26 May with SA Lines of Communication Area Workshops. In June, Queensland: 7 Technical Maintenance Section, attached Advanced LHQ Signals, and Linemen Mechanics Course No. 70. By September he was back in Adelaide for discharge. 28 November 1944, Wayville, medically unfit.

The evidence suggests: his ship was the Nieuw Holland very probably

Moran names that Dutch liner. Convoy US 5 left Fremantle on 22 September 1940 with Christiaan Huygens, Indrapoera, Nieuw Holland and Slamat, 4,262 troops, escorted by HMAS Canberra until 2 October. Named by a comrade: 6 Australian Recovery Section

Bert Evans, SX4626, wrote on 8 February 1971 that Cyril was “SX 8216 Cfn C R Phillips of 6 Aust Recovery Section”, and that they served together from June 1940 until September 1942. Cyril had asked him to record and verify the events. The full transcript is on his Virtual War Memorial Australia profile, submitted by Evans’s daughter, Joan Soroka. This page quotes only short lines from it.

What was happening in the war

Italy had entered the war on 10 June 1940. In September the Italian army advanced into Egypt. The British counter-offensive, Operation Compass, opened in December, as this workshop was moving up to the coast. Cyril's papers do not describe a battle. They describe a tradesman posted to the unit that repaired what the battle broke.

Chapter two · October 1940 – March 1941

Egypt and Libya

The unit recovered broken-down and captured vehicles, repaired trucks, guns and watches, and above all kept the few Infantry tanks fit to fight. "I" tank was the British name for them.

What his unit was doing

5 Dec 1940: Main Shop and No. 6 to Amiriya. 16 Dec: Fuka. 23–27 Dec: 6 Recovery Section's advance party at Kilo 104, near Sidi Barrani.

3–5 Jan 1941: Bardia. The attack opened on 3 January, the town fell on 4 January, and the last positions surrendered on 5 January. Evans recalls Cyril’s blacksmithing on damaged tanks for the move on Tobruk. Those tanks were very probably among the captured Italian tanks painted with white kangaroos by the 6th Division Cavalry.

21–22 Jan: the 6th Division captures Tobruk. 24 Jan: Main Shop to Ain el Gazala. Private E. O. J. Dunning, SX9731, of the same workshops, received the Military Medal, gazetted 8 July 1941. His section is not confirmed.

February: 6 Recovery Section via Giovanni Berta and Tecnis. Main Shop at Maddalena, near Barce. 9–29 March: Mersa Matruh, 432 jobs, then Amiriya.

AWM52 14/2/2/2 to 14/2/2/5. Moran pp. 13–22, 36.

The evidence suggests: not a fixed Christmas cannot be placed

He was in 6 Recovery Section, but an advance party was at Kilo 104 from 23 December, so Fuka is not proved. The Mongareya Tavern photograph is the unit’s Christmas. It does not name him. In March, Evans, the R&I clerk, recalled 140 sets of springs set up at Mersa Matruh. The diary’s figure for the section is 432 jobs. The 140 stays his memory.

Based on: Moran p. 13. AWM52 14/2/2/2. AWM P01522.001 and P01522.003.

Chapter three · April – July 1941

Tobruk: the siege and "the windmill"

On 5 April No. 6 Recovery Section left Amiriya for Tobruk. It arrived before Rommel's Afrika Korps closed the fortress. Moran: "The other absent member, '6 Recovery Section', was besieged in Tobruk. Their site was at the windmill and with craftsmen of the 2/1 Army Field Workshops and the LADs helped in the successful defence of the fortress."

What his unit was doing

8 April: 6 Recovery Section, under Captain J. W. Redpath, in Tobruk. 11 April: "No. 6 Recovery Section moved out to position on Derna Road about 1 mile West of Tobruk." 22 April: Redpath writes from Tobruk that the section has had no losses.

11 July: “Capt. Redpath and No. 6 Rec. Sec. marched in from Tobruk” to Barbara, Palestine. That is the arrival the diary records. The night they left the fortress is a shorter window: he was back with his unit inside Tobruk on 17 June, and the section was at Barbara on 11 July, so they sailed between 17 June and about the first week of July.

AWM52 14/2/1/4 (2/1 AFW, 8 and 11 April). AWM52 14/2/2/5 and 14/2/2/6. Moran pp. 36, 41. The earlier booklet read the same July diary as the section leaving Tobruk on 7–11 July. This edition keeps 11 July as the Barbara march-in, and dates the embarkation from the hospital entry.

From his own record

14 June 1941: admitted to "4 Aust Gen Hosp" with influenza. 17 June: discharged (enteritis) and rejoined his unit the same day.

NAA p. 5.

Inside the siege: now established

Evans’s account, the section’s chronology, the 14–17 June admission to 4 AGH inside the fortress, and the Rats of Tobruk Association register, which lists him by name and number with a note referring to 4 AGH in Tobruk. He worked, Evans says, from daylight until dark on the garrison’s vehicles, living in dugouts under air raids. The earlier “about 70–75%” estimate is retired.

Based on: Evans, 8 February 1971, VWMA transcript. NAA p. 5. AWM52. Rats of Tobruk Association register.

The evidence suggests: the destroyer “Stewart” strong candidate

Evans recalls a night embarkation in an RAN destroyer he names “Stewart”, then a train toward Alexandria that was strafed, with men killed. The ship intended is almost certainly HMAS Stuart, leader of the Scrap Iron Flotilla. She joined the Tobruk Ferry in June 1941 and made 24 runs in June and July. That window fits. It is not proved. A footnote on the transcript says “mid-July”, which conflicts with the diary. A train “to Alex” suggests they were landed at Mersa Matruh, the forward railhead. Evans’s “nearly four months” is about three months: early April to early July.

Based on: Evans. Sea Power Centre – Australia, HMAS Stuart (I). Dates bracketed by NAA p. 5 and AWM52 14/2/2/6.

The evidence suggests: where "the windmill" was moderate

Moran does not say where the windmill stood. The only fix is the diary's "Derna Road about 1 mile West of Tobruk". Most likely a wind-pump or well-head on or near that road, just west of the town and well inside the perimeter. No wartime map marking it has yet been found.

Based on: AWM52 14/2/1/4, 11 April 1941. Moran p. 41. Still to check: AWM Tobruk maps (LIB52519, and the Italian 1:50,000 "Tobruch" sheet) and the 2/1 AFW diaries for May–June 1941.

242days the Australian War Memorial gives for the siege of the fortress, April to the night of 7 December 1941
94days from 8 April, when the diary places 6 Recovery Section in Tobruk, to 11 July, when it marched in at Barbara

The 94 days are the section's recorded stay, not a count of days proved for Cyril. Other histories end the siege on 27 November (231 days), when the garrison first linked up with the Eighth Army. The section had already left in July. The 242 is background, from the Memorial's dating.

What was happening in the war

From 10–11 April 1941 the land route was cut. The 9th Division and the 7th Division’s 18th Brigade formed the core of the garrison. Most Australians were relieved by sea between August and October, and the siege was lifted by Operation Crusader in late November. Supplied by sea: the “Tobruk ferry”. The men took the name Rats of Tobruk after enemy propaganda used it. Cyril is on the association register. The name is not a medal.

Chapter five · March – November 1942

Ceylon, home, and a new army

What his unit was doing

Embarked at Suez in Westernland, Clan McTavish, Gogra and Cromarty. Sailed 13 March 1942, called at Aden, Colombo 27 March. His card says embarked 12 March and does not name the ship. The raid on Colombo followed their arrival. Camp at "Maddagedera", as Moran spells it.

From his own record

13 August–6 September: leave. 15 September: to 6 Aust Div Ordnance Workshop. 3 November: entrains for Queensland.

NAA pp. 5, 6, 18. AWM52 14/2/2/9, "the period 13.7.42 to 7.8.42 having been spent at sea".

What his unit was doing

After Melbourne: camp at Mangalore, Victoria, a march through the city with the 17th Brigade, then Minimbah and Greta in the Hunter. There the old army workshop was broken into a divisional workshop of a headquarters and two brigade-group workshops. On 15 September, the day his transfer took effect, No. 1 Group moved from Minimbah to Greta.

A note on the word "group"

The 6 Div Ordnance Workshop had a headquarters and two brigade group workshops, called No. 1 Group and No. 2 Group in the diary. By mid-1943 his record says "16 Bde Sec 6 Aust Div W/S", the section supporting the 16th Infantry Brigade. Those sections later became numbered brigade workshops. He joined the 2/121st in July 1943.

Do not confuse that with "Group I Blacksmith", which was his pay grade as a skilled tradesman.

AWM52 14/2/2/9. NAA p. 6. Moran p. 47.

The evidence suggests: No. 1 Group at Greta moderate

The transfer date is the day No. 1 Group moved to Greta, and by 1943 he was in the 16 Bde Section. The diary does not say, in the pages seen, that No. 1 Group was the 16th Brigade group.

Chapter six · December 1942 – February 1944

Papua

He disembarked in New Guinea on 12 December 1942, during the closing beachhead battles, and stayed fourteen months. Port Moresby was in the Territory of Papua, which is why this chapter is named Papua. From 1 December 1942 the workshops were AEME, and a private in those units was a craftsman. In September 1943 his mother already wrote “Cfn”.

From his own record

12 December 1942: disembarks in New Guinea with the 16 Bde Section, 6 Aust Div Workshop.

12 July 1943: taken on by 2/121 Aust Bde Workshops from that section.

5–23 September 1943: 128 AGH (NYD, not yet diagnosed), then the New Guinea Details Depot, then back to 2/121 on 23 September.

The evidence suggests: the Port Moresby area strongly indicated

The record’s destination is only “New Guinea”. Port Moresby is the strongest reconstruction: 128 AGH served there until August 1944, 2/1 AGH was at Bootless Bay from September 1943, the ordnance field park at Port Moresby lists the workshops, and Manunda embarked him there. Show it as a probable service area, not a confirmed landing port.

Based on: NAA pp. 6, 18. AWM52 13/40/13/1. Hospital histories. Does not prove: that he never went elsewhere, that the workshop stood in the town, or that the whole of 2/121 Brigade Workshops was there. The missing war diaries leave Wau or Milne Bay possible as short detachments.

The evidence suggests: what the 2/121st supported moderate

The 2/121 Brigade Ordnance Field Park had previously been the "17 Bde Section, 6 Div OFP". That suggests the 2/121st brigade units, the workshop included, were tied to the 6th Division's 17th Brigade, which fought at Wau and Salamaua in 1943. It is not confirmed for the workshop itself.

Based on: AWM52 13/40/13/1, title and opening entries.

Home, discharge, and the six medals

The earlier booklet’s 691 made the parts add to 1,128. The official overseas total is 1,129, and 692 + 437 is that total.

NAA pp. 1, 5–8, 14, 17, 18, 21.

His last papers call him "Sig Phillips", from that final signals posting. In 1977 the Office of Australian War Graves recorded him as a Craftsman, Corps of Electrical and Mechanical Engineers. The workshops had become RAEME in 1948.

Worth knowing for the family

The card is the evidence of the six awards. His service fits each one: the 1939–45 Star for operational service in the desert, Tobruk and Papua; the Africa Star for Egypt and Libya; the Pacific Star for Papua from 12 December 1942; the War Medal for his full-time service; the Australia Service Medal under the original 18-month rule of 1949 (the 1996 reduction to 30 days is irrelevant to him). The Defence Medal is very probably the year in Palestine, Transjordan, Lebanon and Ceylon, plus the voyages. The exact Army reckoning of that medal is not on the file. The Rats of Tobruk Association lists him. That is not a seventh medal.

His service record

Press a stamp

These are the abbreviations a grandchild meets in the file. The stamp opens the note. The scans are the pages themselves. Select a photograph to enlarge it, then pinch or use the zoom buttons.

SX8216

His regimental number. Reading the number, as a guide rather than a line in the file: the first letter is the state of enlistment, S for South Australia, and X marked a man of the Second AIF. 8216 is his own number. It is on the attestation, the casualty form, the medal card and his mother's letter.

Group I Blacksmith, later Group I Fitter

Skilled tradesmen were paid an extra rate by trade group. Group I was the top grade. He was a Group I Blacksmith from enlistment. On 1 February 1944 he was re-graded Group I Fitter. In a field workshop the blacksmiths forged and welded: springs, axles, recovery gear, oxy-acetylene cutting. Fitters machined and assembled parts. "Ins" after Fitter is not spelled out in the file. It may mean instrument.

This is not "No. 1 Group", which was a brigade workshop at Greta. See the glossary.

Cfn, Craftsman

The rank AEME used instead of Private. His mother's letter of September 1943 already says Cfn. The 1977 war-graves card says Craftsman.

4 AGH, and the other hospitals

"4 Aust Gen Hosp" in June 1941 is the 2/4th Australian General Hospital of the Second AIF. The booklet's Tobruk inference depends on that hospital being inside the siege. Later he is in 128 AGH (Port Moresby), 2/1 AGH (Bootless Bay), 115 AGH (Heidelberg), 121 AGH (Northfield) and 105 AMH (Adelaide). AGH is Australian General Hospital. AMH is Australian Military Hospital.

NYD, and the X list

NYD is "not yet diagnosed", written on an admission before the doctors had decided. Men temporarily away from their unit, usually in hospital, went to the X list and were struck off it when they returned. His June 1941 and September 1943 absences are that kind of entry.

Medical classes

Class I at enlistment meant fit for general service. In 1944 the boards moved him through Class B (fit for limited duties), Temporary D, and a final Class D on 17 October: unfit for service. That is why the discharge on 28 November says medically unfit. The illness named at Heidelberg is essential hypertension, high blood pressure.

HMT, and the ships that are named

HMT is His Majesty's Transport, a requisitioned troopship. His record names three ships: HMT Ekma into Melbourne in August 1942, the Duntroon from Townsville in December 1942, and the hospital ship Manunda from Port Moresby in February 1944. The 1940 ship is not named. The March 1942 ship, of the four the unit used, is not named either.

AAOC, AEME, RAEME

The workshops began in the Australian Army Ordnance Corps. On 1 December 1942 they transferred to the Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers. In 1948 that corps became the Royal Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers. His graves card uses the later title. A LAD was a light aid detachment, a small repair team with a fighting unit. An OFP was an ordnance field park, the spare-parts column. L of C was a Lines of Communication Area, the state administrative command. LHQ was Land Headquarters. 1 ATTD is left as the file writes it.

The pages

A guide to the records

What the trade terms mean

Army Field Workshop

A large mobile repair unit for a corps. The 2/2nd had a Main Shop, with machine-tool trailers and engineering, transport, instrument and radio sections, and three Recovery Sections, Nos. 4, 5 and 6.

Recovery Section

The most mobile part. It brought in broken, bogged, damaged or captured vehicles, tanks and guns, often close to the fighting, and repaired what it could on the spot. No. 6 was raised in South Australia.

Group I

The top tradesman's pay grade. Not the same as No. 1 Group, which was a workshop sub-unit at Greta in 1942.

Blacksmith and fitter

Blacksmiths forged and welded. Fitters machined and assembled. "Ins" beside Fitter may mean instrument. The file does not say.

Craftsman

From 1 December 1942, AEME’s title for a private. Not a promotion. From 1943 his papers say Cfn. In 1977 the graves card says Craftsman, Corps of Electrical and Mechanical Engineers.

Female Relative Badge

Inscribed “To the Women of Australia”, issued from 1940 to the nearest female relative of a man serving abroad. This is the “broach” his mother requested. The file shows her letter received on 8 September 1943 and marked “Badge sent”.

AAOC, AEME, RAEME

Ordnance Corps, then from 1 December 1942 the Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers, then from 1948 the Royal Australian Electrical and Mechanical Engineers.

Brigade workshop

After 1942 the big workshops were split for jungle war into brigade groups, later numbered. The 2/121st is his from July 1943.

LAD and OFP

A light aid detachment lived with a combat unit. An ordnance field park carried the spare parts. In Tobruk, recovery sections and LADs worked side by side. That is Moran's sentence, not a claim about a particular hour of Cyril's.

X list and NYD

X list: away from the unit, usually in hospital. NYD: not yet diagnosed.

Classes A to D

Fitness. Class B, limited duties. Class D, unfit, which is the discharge.

L of C, HMT, LHQ

Lines of Communication Area. His Majesty's Transport. Land Headquarters. 4 Military District was South Australia's army district, the one he marched into in July 1940.

"As read"

Where the booklet could not be sure of a handwritten word, it says so. Kantara, the Ekma date, and "Posted 8/4/54" are the three to remember.
